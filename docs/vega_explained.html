Vega, explained

The same diagrams as the short page, with the explanation written out under each one: what the ball and the valleys are, which field of physics Vega borrows from, which other fields have fed machine learning, and how Vega compares with Laya on the same test.

The idea in one paragraph

Vega is a decision model: you give it a piece of text and a question whose answers are fixed in advance, and it returns one answer with a probability. Inside, the engine is a simulation of a ball on a landscape. Each possible answer is a valley. The command and question, read by a frozen language model, set where the ball starts, how deep and wide each valley is, and give it a small push. The engine then runs twelve steps of damped motion with friction, the same equations as a marble losing energy as it rolls, and the ball comes to rest in one valley. Which valley is the answer; how deep it sits and how fast it settled give the confidence and the abstain flag. A ball that stops nowhere near any valley raises the "unbound" flag. The physics is the mechanism, not the subject matter: nothing about the text is physical.

Where the landscape comes from

"Reads the text" is a plain pooling step. The command, the question and each answer option are written into one prompt in a fixed format. The frozen language model runs forward only as far as its nineteenth layer (of 24 in the 0.8B); the language-modelling head that would generate words never executes. At layers 13 and 19 the token vectors are averaged over each span: the situation, the question, each option separately, and the last token of the prompt. Those five vectors are all the engine ever sees.

The small trained networks take those vectors and produce the landscape. One network places the ball's start point and another its first push. A third reads each option's pooled vector and emits the centre, depth and width of that option's valley, so a label set the model has never seen still gets a valley per label. Two more are consulted at every step of the roll: one sets the friction, one adds a small learned nudge, both reading the trajectory so far. The bowl that stops the ball drifting away is fixed. Because the language model never changes, adapting Vega to your data means retraining only these small networks, or a 0.84M-parameter adapter on top of them, or fitting a separate head on the five vectors.

What the roll gives you that a plain classifier does not: three quantities that fall out of the mechanics for free. How much speed is left and how far the ball sits from the valley floor say how settled the answer is, and Vega's calibration reads exactly those to set its confidence instead of trusting a number the network was trained to emit. The distance from the nearest valley centre says whether the question was out of its depth at all; past a fitted threshold the answer is marked unbound. And a stability condition from control theory, a Lyapunov term in the training loss, guarantees that friction always wins, so the ball settles instead of circling forever. In my own test these were the reason Vega 4B's probabilities came out more honest than Laya's.

Two things make this more than a metaphor. First, nothing about the text is physical: the 64 dimensions have no meaning, and the valleys are rebuilt from scratch for every question from the option text, which is why any label set works without retraining. Second, the only trained parts are the small networks that turn text features into valley positions, depths, widths, the start point, the friction and the nudge: 14M parameters in the 0.8B, 31M in the 4B. The integration itself has no parameters at all. That split, trained landscape and parameter-free motion, is what lets confidence and abstention be read off physical quantities instead of learned.

The field Vega belongs to

The field is usually called physics-informed or dynamics-based neural networks. The shared idea: instead of letting a network map input to output in one jump, you make it describe how a state moves over time, and you borrow the equations physicists use for motion so the movement behaves well.

Neural ODEs (2018, Chen and others). A normal deep network is a stack of discrete layers. A neural ODE replaces the stack with one small network that says "here is the velocity of the state right now", and an ODE solver integrates that velocity forward in time. Depth becomes continuous time. You get adaptive compute (the solver takes as many steps as the problem needs), constant memory, and the ability to model irregularly timed data such as medical records. Vega's "up to 12 steps of integration, stopping when the ball settles" is this idea with a fixed step budget.

Hamiltonian neural networks (2019, Greydanus and others). A plain neural ODE can learn any velocity field, including ones that leak or create energy out of nothing, so long simulations drift. A Hamiltonian network learns a single scalar function, the total energy, and derives the motion from it using Hamilton's equations: position changes according to the energy's slope in momentum, momentum changes according to the energy's slope in position. Anything integrated this way conserves energy and is reversible by construction. Trained on a pendulum or a two-body orbit, it stays on the right orbit for thousands of steps where a plain network wanders off. Vega's engine keeps position and momentum as separate quantities and updates momentum before position, the symplectic integration scheme these networks use.

Lagrangian neural networks (2020, Cranmer and others). Same goal, different starting point. Instead of energy as a function of position and momentum, you learn the Lagrangian (kinetic minus potential energy) as a function of position and velocity, and derive motion from the principle of least action. The practical gain is working in ordinary coordinates such as angles without converting to momenta first.

Where Vega sits, and where it departs. It learns a potential energy landscape: a bowl, so the ball cannot escape, minus one Gaussian well per candidate answer, with the wells' positions and depths produced from the text. It integrates with momentum, like a Hamiltonian system. Then it deliberately breaks the conservation rule: it adds learned friction so the ball loses energy and comes to rest. A pure Hamiltonian system never stops; a decision needs a stopping point. In physics terms it is a damped system, and the training's Lyapunov term is the control-theory guarantee that damping always wins.

Why anyone does this. Three things come free from the physics that a plain classifier has to learn from data: a built-in notion of how settled an answer is, a natural out-of-distribution signal (the ball stopped nowhere), and stability under long computation.

Cousins you may meet. Deep equilibrium models (2019) run a layer until its output stops changing, the same "settle" idea without the mechanics vocabulary. Physics-informed neural networks, PINNs (2019), go the other way: a network that must satisfy a known physical equation, used for fluid or heat simulation rather than classification. Diffusion models are the thermodynamic branch of the same family tree.

Other fields that have fed machine learning

  • Thermodynamics: diffusion models, the engine behind today's image generators, copy a gas spreading out and run it in reverse (Sohl-Dickstein and others, 2015).
  • Statistical physics: Hopfield networks and modern energy-based models; Hopfield's 2024 Nobel Prize was for exactly this borrowing (Hopfield, 1982).
  • Classical mechanics: Hamiltonian and Lagrangian neural networks, neural ODEs; Vega sits in this family.
  • Control theory: Mamba and state-space models came out of signal processing and control; Vega's Lyapunov term is from the same shelf.
  • Electrostatics: Poisson flow generative models treat data points as electric charges.
  • Neuroscience: liquid neural networks modelled on a worm's nervous system, spiking networks, and memory layers built around "surprise".
  • Statistics and forecasting: Laya and Jev train with proper scoring rules, the Brier-score idea weather forecasters use to keep probabilities honest.
  • Metallurgy: simulated annealing, still used for tuning, copies the slow cooling of metal.

Each time one of these appears, two fields that developed their tools separately turn out to have been describing the same thing. A physicist's "settle into the lowest energy state" is a machine learner's "choose the most likely answer", and once you see it, the physicist's two hundred years of theorems about settling become yours to use.

Laya and Vega side by side

Laya (September 2026, Convai Innovations) is a 421M-parameter decision model: ModernBERT-large reads the text and a two-layer head with an option scorer decides, trained with reinforcement learning against proper scoring rules. Every parameter is trained. Vega (October 2026, same author) keeps the reader frozen and trains only the engine. Vega 0.8B reads with Qwen3.5-0.8B, an 800-million-parameter language model, and decides with a 14M-parameter engine (57 MB); Vega 4B reads with Qwen3.5-4B and decides with a 31M-parameter engine (124 MB). Same code, same questions, same answer format. The author keeps the 0.8B as the default because it is faster and smaller, and says the 4B is the better model on nearly everything he measured. In my test the bigger reader mattered more than the engine: zero-shot, the 4B went from 82% to 95% balanced accuracy on the short set and from AUROC 0.63 to 0.82 on my labelled real commands, at four times the latency.

The last row is the one to read carefully. Vega's fitted head catches the most dangerous commands for the same number of harmless pauses, but it only hits those numbers if you hand it the cut-off; it cannot pick one itself. Laya's fine-tune and Vega's adapter can. Twenty dangerous commands per set means one command moves a number by five points, so treat "2 against 4 missed" as a lead, not a proof.

Where Vega is worse than Laya: asked what a command mainly does, it labels commands like cat and ls as "sends data out", so the reason shown with a stop would be wrong more often; and it is 4 to nearly 40 times slower on a Mac.
